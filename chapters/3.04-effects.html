3.4 Effects

How do we measure effects and how do we interpret them?

You arrive here from Correlation & Covariance and Probability & Sampling, so you can already quantify how two variables relate and you understand that samples vary by chance. Here you’ll learn what an effect* is, how to express its size on a standardized scale (Cohen’s \(d\), \(r\)), and why “how big is the effect?” is a different question from “how sure are we that it’s not zero?”. Confusing these two is one of the most common mistakes in reading (and reporting) research.*

3.4.1 What is an “effect”?

Whenever a study compares groups, or relates two variables, it is looking for an effect: some systematic pattern in the data, as opposed to pure noise. Effects come in several familiar shapes, most of which you’ve already met:

  • A mean difference: newspaper subscribers score, on average, higher on political interest than non-subscribers.
  • A correlation: more news consumption goes together with more political interest.
  • A regression slope: for every extra hour of news consumption per week, political interest goes up by some amount.

All of these answer the same underlying question — “does X relate to Y, and how strongly?” — just using different kinds of variables.

3.4.2 Raw effects are hard to compare

Suppose newspaper subscribers score 7.12 on average on a political-interest scale, versus 5.06 for non-subscribers — a difference of 2.06 points. Is that a big effect? It depends entirely on the scale the variable was measured on, and on how much people vary to begin with. A 2-point difference on a 1–10 scale is substantial; the same 2-point difference would be tiny on a 1–100 scale. This is the same problem you already saw with covariance in Correlation & Covariance: raw, unstandardized numbers depend on the units of measurement, which makes them hard to compare across studies or variables.

The solution is the same one you already know: standardize.

3.4.3 Cohen’s d: a standardized mean difference

Cohen’s \(d\) expresses a mean difference between two groups in standard-deviation units, instead of raw scale points. The formula is:

\[ d = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{SD_{pooled}} \]

where \(SD_{pooled}\) is a (sample-size weighted) average of the standard deviations in the two groups. A simplified version, when the two groups are roughly the same size, just averages the two SDs:

d <- tibble(
  np_subscription  = c(rep("yes", 5), rep("no", 5)),
  political_interest = c(7.2, 6.8, 7.5, 7.0, 7.1, 5.1, 4.8, 5.5, 5.0, 4.9)
)

group_stats <- d |>
  group_by(np_subscription) |>
  summarise(m = mean(political_interest), s = sd(political_interest))

group_stats
mean_diff <- group_stats$m[group_stats$np_subscription == "yes"] -
             group_stats$m[group_stats$np_subscription == "no"]

pooled_sd <- mean(group_stats$s)

cohens_d <- mean_diff / pooled_sd
cohens_d
[1] 7.787857

A common (but not universal) rule of thumb, also due to Cohen, is that \(d = 0.2\) is a small effect, \(d = 0.5\) is medium, and \(d = 0.8\) is large. Our example above, with \(d \approx 7.79\), would be considered a very large effect — but keep in mind this is a tiny, illustrative dataset, and real-world effects are rarely this clean.

If you’d rather not compute this by hand every time, the effectsize package provides cohens_d(), which also returns a confidence interval around the estimate:

library(effectsize)
cohens_d(political_interest ~ np_subscription, data = d)

3.4.4 Correlation (r) as an effect size

When your effect is a relationship between two continuous variables rather than a group difference, the correlation coefficient \(r\) you already met in Correlation & Covariance already is a standardized effect size — that’s precisely why we bothered to standardize the covariance in the first place. The same rough rule-of-thumb thresholds are often used: around \(r = 0.1\) is small, \(0.3\) is medium, and \(0.5\) is large.

Other common standardized effect sizes you will encounter later in the book include \(R^2\) (the proportion of variance explained by a regression model) and the odds ratio (how much the odds of an event change, used for binary outcomes in logistic regression). The details differ, but the goal is always the same: express an effect on a scale that doesn’t depend on the units of the original variables.

3.4.5 Effect size \(\neq\) statistical significance

This is the single most important distinction in this chapter. A \(p\)-value (which you’ll study properly in Hypothesis Testing) answers “how likely is it that I’d see a difference this big (or bigger) purely by chance, if there were truly no effect in the population?” An effect size answers a completely different question: “how big is the difference, regardless of how sure we are about it?”

Here’s why this matters in practice:

  • With a very large sample, even a tiny, practically meaningless effect (say, \(d = 0.02\)) can become “statistically significant” (\(p < .05\)), simply because large samples make estimates very precise. The effect is detectable, but it may not be worth caring about.
  • With a very small sample, even a large, practically important effect can fail to reach statistical significance, simply because there isn’t enough data to be confident it isn’t due to chance.

So a responsible reading of any result always asks both questions: is this effect likely to be real (significance), and is this effect big enough to matter (effect size)? Good research reports both, and journals increasingly require it. How to plan a study so that it has a good chance of detecting an effect of a given size is the subject of Power Analysis, later in the book.
